summary: >-
  Verifying a multimeter's DC voltage indication against applied values of
  known uncertainty, across every range in the reported scope. The bench work
  that most calibration careers are built on, and the function where technique
  is most exposed: on the millivolt ranges the thermal EMF of the connection is
  comparable to what is being judged, so a verification done carelessly
  measures the leads rather than the meter and produces a record that looks
  identical either way.
levelCeiling: 4
anchors:
  1: >-
    With the calibrator and meter already connected and the test points
    specified, applies each point in turn, allows the indication to settle,
    records what the meter displays, and computes the deviation from the
    applied value in the units of the measurement with the sign carried.
  2: >-
    Makes the connection themselves from a supplied procedure, works through
    the specified points across the ranges including both polarities and a
    shorted-input zero, records the as-found indications, and states for each
    point whether the deviation falls within the meter's permitted error at
    that point.
  3: >-
    For a meter they have not calibrated before, selects the test points across
    and within the ranges from the meter's specification and the laboratory's
    scope, makes a connection that keeps thermal EMF below the level that
    matters and demonstrates that it does by recording the shorted-input
    indication, produces the as-found record, and records why those points were
    chosen so a reviewer can follow the selection.
  4: >-
    Handles the case the procedure does not cover — a range where the available
    standard cannot verify the meter's specification at an adequate ratio, or a
    meter whose indication drifts across the dwell so that no single reading
    represents it — produces a defensible treatment and record, and defends it
    against a reviewer probing whether the result was reported as a clean pass
    that the numbers do not support, including saying what would change the
    approach.

Notes for assessors and item authors. The subject is explained in `BOK-0006` —
do not restate it here.

**This is the first `demonstration: [equipment]` element in the corpus, and the
distinction from `kind` is the thing to keep straight.** `skill` says the
evidence is a work product rather than an explanation. `equipment` says the
work product cannot be produced at a desk. `CM-03-052` is `skill` and `desk`;
this element is `skill` and `equipment`, and the difference is what a learner
without bench access is actually blocked on. An account of how one *would*
verify a DC voltage function is a knowledge claim wearing a skill's clothes and
does not evidence this element at any level.

**The barrier here is real and must not be softened.** Rule 11 forbids
inventing a barrier; it equally forbids hiding one. A module preparing for this
element lists it in `requiresPhysicalDemonstration` and leaves it
`pending-demonstration`, and a learner should be told that plainly at the
start rather than discovering it at assessment.

**The levels move on how much of the method is the candidate's.** At L1 the
connection exists and the points are given, so what is observed is careful
execution and correct sign convention. At L2 they build the connection and
work a supplied list. At L3 there is no list — the points come from the
specification and the scope, and the recorded justification becomes the
assessable artifact alongside the data. At L4 the method does not reach the
case.

**Where candidates fail, in order of frequency.** Reading immediately rather
than letting the indication settle, which on the millivolt ranges records a
thermal transient as a deviation. Omitting the shorted-input zero, so nothing
in the record shows whether the connection was good enough for the low points
to mean anything — this is the single most common gap and it is invisible
afterwards. Testing only near full scale, which exercises the gain term and
leaves the offset term almost unmeasured. Losing the sign on the deviation,
which discards whether the meter reads high or low. Recording a pass rather
than the deviation and the permitted error as separate figures, so a later
reader who disagrees with the specification column cannot redo the comparison.
And touching the terminals immediately before a low-range reading.

**The L3/L4 boundary.** L3 is a competent verification of an unfamiliar meter
where the standard is adequate and the instrument behaves. L4 is where one of
those fails: the ratio does not hold on some range, or the meter will not sit
still. The defensible answers are documented — use a better standard, restrict
the reported scope, report with a guardband, or characterize the drift and say
so — and the failure the level is written to catch is reporting a clean pass
anyway. An item at L4 answerable by working carefully through the standard
procedure is an L3 item.

**A cross-axis prerequisite, deliberately.** `DP-08-064` is a `DP` element and
this is an `EC` element, and the dependency is real: a candidate who cannot
compute a permitted error from a specification cannot say whether any point
passed. This is the first element to cross axes in its prerequisite graph, and
it is worth noticing that the `EC` axis will do this constantly — equipment
competence rests on quantity competence almost everywhere.
