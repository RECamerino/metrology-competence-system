summary: >-
  Deciding what a later reader will need, when you do not know who they are and
  cannot ask. No clause specifies this and a record can comply fully with every
  requirement and still be unusable — written in a shared vocabulary the reader
  does not have, leaning on context that was in the room. The competence is
  noticing which of your own entries only make sense to you, which is
  intrinsically hard because they read perfectly to the person who wrote them.

  2: >-
    For a supplied record and a stated next reader, decides which entries carry

  3: >-

    that reader is, records the assumption about the reader the writing rests

    different audience — conceding the entries that genuinely do not survive
    the change of reader.

Notes for assessors and item authors. The subject is explained in `BOK-0007`
§s05 — do not restate it here.

**This element is the corpus's test of its own second rule, and it is worth
saying so.** Rule 2 requires a clause-level source reference on everything and
explicitly does not require the source to be normative, because the corpus
covers adjacent competencies "where no clause requires anything of anybody".
This is one of those. The citations here are honest and none of them requires
what the element assesses: `7.8.1.2` asks for results to be unambiguous and
says nothing about how, which is the whole gap. `authorityStatus:
accepted-practice` carries that fact in data rather than in prose.

**A `judgment` element, because there is no correct answer available in
advance.** What a reader needs depends on who they are, the author usually does
not know, and two competent practitioners will draw the line differently for
the same record. What is assessed is the decision and the defence of it — which
is why the L3 anchor requires the *assumption about the reader* to be recorded.
An unstated audience assumption is what makes a record unreviewable: a reviewer
cannot say whether the writing serves its reader without knowing who the writer
thought that was.

**Item design has one genuine difficulty.** The competence is noticing that
your own writing depends on context you have. A candidate reviewing *somebody
else's* record has an unfair advantage — the missing context is missing for
them too, so the defects are visible. That shape tests something real but
easier, and it belongs at L2. L3 must have the candidate produce the record,
because self-blindness is the actual competence and it cannot be observed on
another person's work.

**Where candidates fail.** Producing a record that is complete under
`CM-15-046` and still unreadable, which is the failure the element exists for.
Defending every entry under challenge instead of conceding the ones that do not
survive a change of reader — the L3 anchor names conceding explicitly, as
`CM-03-040` does. Writing for an assessor when the next reader is a colleague,
which produces bloat and is a real cost rather than a safe default. And
recording departures at the end of the day rather than as they happen, which is
where a technical note turns into something that reads like an excuse.

**`technical-assessor` is rated 2 rather than 3**, which looks anomalous beside
the other senior roles and is deliberate. An assessor's job is reading records
and judging whether they serve a reader — L2 is exactly that. Producing records
under an unknown audience is not what the role does, and rating it 3 would
demand a competence the role has no occasion to exercise.
